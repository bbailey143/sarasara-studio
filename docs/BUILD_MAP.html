Leg 01 · closed

Oil went in, and you signed off on five of its behaviours.

This was the first test that could actually have proved you wrong. Thirteen things oil must do — twelve already had a word for them. The thirteenth turned out to be the piece your other two materials were stuck behind.

5 behaviours you approved by eye

It holds its shape, the brush shoves it, it never wets the sheet, it never bleeds, and a near-empty brush drags colour out onto bare canvas. Twelve of oil’s thirteen behaviours already had a word in your vocabulary — no new family was needed, which was the test. Two rounds of your review found real faults: the brush was scraping the sheet clean, and it could not carry paint at all. Both fixed, both re-approved.

The one thing missing: paint that stands up off the sheet

Nothing in the vocabulary says how tall deposited material sits. Watercolor lies flat and charcoal lies flat, so it never came up. Oil is the whole point of impasto.

But height isn't a property of the paint — it's something the sheet works out from what it's already holding:

relief height = deposited mass ÷ (packing × particle density × area)

All three inputs already exist. So this is one derived value added inside an existing family — bookkeeping, not a new idea. The vocabulary already allows exactly this.

and it unblocks Charcoal burnishing — rubbing it slick until it stops taking more. Needs a layer whose packing changes.
and it unblocks Watercolor glazing — real layers with real thickness, stacked in order.
and it unblocks The drying edge — a rim is a change in thickness, not a change in colour.

The finding that matters: oil didn't ask for special treatment. It exposed one thing the bench never had — a deposited layer with real thickness that can be pushed — and three behaviours on your other two materials were already stuck behind it. That's the best outcome a test like this can have. Written up in full at docs/decisions/ADR-0002.

One bench — the shared physics

A material is a card of settings you slot in. New material, no new machine — the way a paint maker changes the binder, not the factory.

01 · substrate The Sheet What receives. Tooth, thirst, weave. Chosen separately from the paint — as in life.
02 · applicator The Hand What carries and presses. Pressure, speed, load, run-out.
03 · material The Recipe What the stuff is made of. Carrier, pigment, binder, grain, brittleness.
04 · solver The Bench Where all three meet. One set of rules, no favourites, no secret doors.
05 · conservation The Ledger Every speck accounted for. This is what stops the app from faking a nice mark.

Staying oriented

How this sheet stops you getting lost again.

It only works because it's part of the build, not a thing made once and abandoned.

The board is live in the bench

Set marks where the looking happens — in the studio, as you paint. They write to docs/validation/board.json. This sheet stays the wide view; the bench is the live one.

Sessions record themselves

Every saved mark writes settings, measurements, your words and a picture into docs/validation/sessions/. Nothing gets retyped to reach me.

Only you turn a mark green

Machine-checked gets a half mark and stops there. No amount of passing tests advances a behaviour past your eye.

It's the briefing for every build

Work starts by naming the leg and the row it serves. If a task doesn't map to a row on the board, it isn't on the road.

Your standing prompt — paste this whenever you want the map brought current:

Update the Build Map. Check what's actually true in the repo first —
don't take the handoff's word for it. Move the pin, change any marks
that earned it, rewrite the Next card. Tell me in one line what moved
and why. Nothing turns green unless I said yes to it.

The rule that keeps all of this honest, and the reason you got lost without it: a mark is a claim about what you have seen with your own eyes — never about what the code manages to do.
